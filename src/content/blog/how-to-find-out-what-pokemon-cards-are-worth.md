To find out what a Pokémon card is worth, you need three things: the **exact card**, its **condition**, and **recent sold prices** for that card in that condition. Skip any one of them and the number you get will be wrong, often by a lot.

## Step 1: Identify the exact card

The name on the card is only the start. Charizard has appeared on well over a hundred different cards, priced from pocket change to the cost of a car.

Look at the bottom of the card for:

- **The card number**, such as 4/102.
- **The set symbol or set code**, a small icon or a short code of letters and numbers.
- **The rarity symbol**, a circle, diamond or star.

Together, the set and number pin down which card you have.

## Step 2: Identify the printing

The same card number can exist in several versions:

- **Normal, holo or reverse holo.** Tilt the card under a light to see which parts shine.
- **1st Edition.** A small black stamp with a "1" on early sets.
- **Unlimited.** The standard later print run without the stamp.

These are priced separately. Picking the wrong one is the most common reason people over- or under-value a card.

## Step 3: Judge the condition honestly

Marketplaces describe raw cards on a scale that runs roughly like this:

| Condition | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Near Mint | Looks fresh from the pack. At most a tiny flaw. |
| Lightly Played | Minor edge wear or a few light scratches. |
| Moderately Played | Obvious wear, whitening or scuffing. |
| Heavily Played | Creases, heavy wear, still intact. |
| Damaged | Tears, bends, water damage or writing. |

Check the corners, the edges on the back, and the surface under a bright light. Most cards that have lived in a box or been handled by a child are not Near Mint, and the price drops at each step down.

## Step 4: Check what it actually sells for

There are three places most collectors look:

- **TCGplayer market price.** Based on recent sales on the largest US card marketplace. A good default for raw cards.
- **Cardmarket.** The main marketplace in Europe, useful if you are selling there.
- **eBay sold listings.** Filter by "sold items" to see real completed sales, which is especially useful for graded and vintage cards.

Use sold prices, not asking prices. A listing at $500 means nothing if the last ten copies sold for $80.

## Step 5: Decide whether grading matters

Graded cards, sealed in a case by PSA, BGS or CGC with a score out of 10, sell in their own market. A top grade can multiply the price of a valuable card.

Grading is not free and it is not quick. As a rule of thumb, it is worth considering only when the raw card is already valuable and the condition is genuinely excellent. For everything else, sell it raw.

## Pricing a whole collection

The steps above work for one card. For a binder or a box, doing each card by hand is slow, so most people triage:

1. Pull out holos, full arts, anything with a 1st Edition stamp and anything from older sets.
2. Price those individually.
3. Treat the remaining commons and uncommons as bulk unless something stands out.

This is where a scanner earns its place. With Poké Pricer you can scan a stack card by card, see a market price for each as you go, and get a running total at the end, which makes the first pass through a collection much faster.

## Keep expectations realistic

A market price is what a card tends to sell for, not a guaranteed payout. Selling fees, shipping and the time it takes to find a buyer all come off the top, and a shop buying your cards will offer less than market so it can resell them. Use the price as a guide to what you have, then decide how much effort the sale is worth.
