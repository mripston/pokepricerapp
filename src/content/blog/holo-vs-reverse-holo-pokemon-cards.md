On a **holo** card, the artwork is shiny and the rest of the card is flat. On a **reverse holo**, it is the other way round: the artwork is flat and the rest of the card has the foil finish. They share a name and a card number, but they are bought and sold as separate cards with separate prices.

## How to tell them apart in five seconds

Tilt the card under a lamp or a window.

- **Light catches the picture only:** holo, sometimes written as holofoil.
- **Light catches everything except the picture:** reverse holo.
- **Nothing shines:** a normal, non-foil card.

Full art and textured cards are their own category. If the artwork covers the whole card and the surface has a fine texture, you are looking at an ultra rare or illustration rare rather than a standard holo.

## Where reverse holos come from

Reverse holos first appeared in the English game with Legendary Collection in 2002, and they have been part of most main sets since. The idea is simple: nearly every common, uncommon and rare in the set gets a second version with foil on the card body.

Most modern booster packs include at least one reverse holo. Because any card in the set can turn up in that slot, a set of 150 cards has roughly 150 extra reverse holo versions to collect. This is why completing a "master set" takes so much longer than completing the basic set list.

## Why the price is different

Same Pokémon, same number, different price. A few things drive the gap:

- **Supply.** There is one particular reverse holo you want and dozens that could come out of the pack instead.
- **Master set collectors.** People finishing a master set need every reverse holo, including commons nobody else is looking for.
- **Age.** Reverse holos from early-2000s sets were printed in far smaller numbers than today and are much harder to find in clean condition.
- **Which version looks better.** Sometimes collectors simply prefer one finish, and the price follows.

For a modern common, the reverse holo might be worth a small amount more than the normal card. For older sets, the gap can be enormous. It runs the other way too: for a holo rare, the standard holo is sometimes the pricier version.

## The most common pricing mistake

Because both versions share a card number, a quick search often returns the wrong one. People look up their card, see a high figure, and only later realise that figure belonged to the reverse holo, or the other way round.

Before you trust any price, confirm three things:

1. **The set**, from the set symbol or set code.
2. **The card number**, bottom corner.
3. **The finish:** normal, holo or reverse holo.

Get all three right and you have the correct printing. Miss the third and you could be out by a factor of ten or more.

## Condition matters more on foil

Foil surfaces show wear easily. Reverse holos in particular pick up fine scratches across the card body, and older ones are prone to edge whitening and print lines. Two copies of the same reverse holo can sell for very different amounts depending on how the surface looks under light, so check it at an angle before you buy or list one.

## What to do with a pile of them

If you have a stack of reverse holos from recent sets, most will be bulk. A handful will not be. The practical approach is to sort by set, pull anything from older sets first, and then price the rest by printing rather than by name.

Poké Pricer lists each finish of a card separately, so when you scan a card you can choose reverse holofoil and see the price for that version rather than a blended guess.
