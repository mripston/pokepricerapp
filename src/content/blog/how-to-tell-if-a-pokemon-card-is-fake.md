To tell if a Pokémon card is fake, **compare it side by side with a card you know is real** and check the back colour, the text, the feel of the cardstock and the way the foil shines. Most fakes fail several of these at once, and you do not need any special equipment to spot them.

## Start with a card you know is real

Every check below is easier with a genuine card in your other hand. Use one you pulled yourself from a pack bought at a proper shop, ideally from the same era as the card you are checking. A basic Energy card is fine.

You are not looking for one decisive flaw. You are looking for a pile of small differences. A real card that came out of the factory slightly off-centre is normal. A card with the wrong blue on the back, soft text and a waxy feel is not.

## The 8 checks

1. **The back.** Lay both cards face down. On a fake, the blue is often too pale, too purple or washed out, and the swirl pattern looks blurry or lacks detail. The back design has barely changed over the years, so this comparison works across most English sets.
2. **The text.** Real cards have crisp, thin, consistent lettering. Fakes often use a font that is slightly too bold or too narrow, with uneven spacing. Look for spelling mistakes, a missing accent on the "é" in Pokémon, and attack text that reads oddly.
3. **The numbers.** Check the HP and attack damage. Fakes sometimes show absurd figures far beyond anything printed on real cards. Then check the card number and set symbol in the bottom corner. Our guide on [how to tell if a Pokémon card is rare](/blog/how-to-tell-if-a-pokemon-card-is-rare/) explains what should be there.
4. **The feel.** Flex the card gently between your fingers. Real cards are stiff and spring back. Fakes tend to be thinner, bend too easily, and feel either slick and waxy or rough like plain paper.
5. **The edge.** Look at the side of the card under good light. Real cards are made from layered cardstock with a thin dark layer in the middle, which you can sometimes see as a faint line. A plain white edge with no layering is a bad sign.
6. **The shine.** Many fakes have a flat rainbow sheen that runs across the whole face of the card, including parts that should not be shiny at all. On a real card the foil sits where it belongs. See [holo vs reverse holo](/blog/holo-vs-reverse-holo-pokemon-cards/) for what each finish should look like.
7. **The texture.** Most modern full art and higher rarity cards have fine ridges you can feel with a fingertip and see when you tilt the card, a bit like a fingerprint. A fake of one of these cards is usually completely smooth and glossy. Note that plenty of real cards are meant to be smooth, so this check only applies to cards that should be textured.
8. **The light.** Hold a phone torch behind each card. Fakes often glow brightly because the stock is thin and has no dark inner layer, while real cards block most of the light. Treat this as a clue, not proof, because results vary between sets and print runs.

## Quick reference: real vs fake

| What to check | Real card | Common fake |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Back colour | Deep, even blue with sharp detail | Pale, purple or blurry |
| Text | Crisp and evenly spaced | Too bold, soft or misspelled |
| Cardstock | Stiff, springs back | Thin, flimsy or waxy |
| Foil | Only where the design calls for it | Flat sheen over the whole card |
| Texture on full arts | Fine ridges you can feel | Smooth and glossy |
| Torch behind the card | Blocks most light | Glows through |

## What about the rip test?

Tearing a card in half shows the dark inner layer clearly on a real one. It also destroys the card. Only do it to something you are happy to throw away, such as one common from a batch you already believe is fake. Never rip a card that could be worth money just to find out.

## Warning signs before you buy

Most fakes are easier to avoid than to detect. Be careful when:

- the price is far below what the same card or pack sells for everywhere else
- the photos are stock images, or are too blurry to read the text
- a listing offers a big bundle of "rare" or gold cards for very little
- sealed packs look loosely crimped, have dull printing or feel light
- the seller will not send a clear photo of the back

For an expensive single card, a copy authenticated by a grading company takes most of the risk out of it.

## If you think a card is fake

If you bought it recently, contact the seller or the marketplace and ask for a refund. Most large marketplaces have buyer protection for counterfeit items, and clear side by side photos help your case. Do not sell or trade the card on as real.

If the card came from an old childhood collection, do not panic over one odd-looking copy. Print quality varied between print runs, and worn cards can look and feel different from fresh ones. Judge it on several checks, not one.

## Checking the card against the real printing

It helps to know exactly what the genuine card should look like. A [Pokémon card scanner](/pokemon-card-scanner/) like Poké Pricer will identify the card and show which printings and finishes exist, so you can confirm that a card with that name, number and set is a real release. It cannot tell you whether your copy is authentic, though. A convincing fake will scan as the card it imitates, so treat the scan as a reference and trust your eyes and fingers for the verdict.
